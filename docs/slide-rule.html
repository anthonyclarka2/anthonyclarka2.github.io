    Why this instrument is interesting

    A slide rule has no gears, no digits and no memory. It is two sticks of wood with numbers printed on them, and it multiplies because the distance from 1 to 2 is the same on every logarithmic scale, so setting one stick's 1 against a number and reading another number against the stock adds their logarithms — which is what multiplication is.

    That single idea buys multiplication, division, squares, roots, cubes, common logarithms, sines and tangents. It buys none of addition, because there is no scale that adds a distance to another.

    Sines are read against D, and surprise most people when they first see it: D carries ten times the sine, so sin 30° sits over D's 5 rather than D's 0.5. The rule is one decade long and a sine never exceeds one, so the reader places the decimal point — which is what an instrument of this period asks of them. There is no cosine scale either: cos 60° is read as sin 30°, the sine of the complement.

    The log-log rows are the cleverest thing on it. They graduate the logarithm of the logarithm, so a single turn of the slide raises a number to any power instead of only squaring and cubing it, and the same rows printed below one run backwards, which is how the rule divides by a number below one in one setting.

    One decade is short. An operand outside 1 to 10 is brought down to its own mantissa and the decades are carried in the reader's head; the narration says so whenever it happens. A product past ten is read against the right-hand index of C instead of the left, and a quotient below one is read the same way round, one place smaller.
